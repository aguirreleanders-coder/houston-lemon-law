New Car Lemon Law in Houston, TX

We review repair records for repeat defects in new vehicles across Houston.

Call (832) 245-5128 Ask a question

An engine warning light that returns after a repair, a transmission noise, a screen that goes black, or a vehicle that stalls after several shop visits gives us a repair history to examine. We look beyond the dashboard symptom to the repair order, the concern written by the service department, and the work performed before the vehicle left the shop. New car lemon law matters can turn on that paper trail as much as the part that failed.

We hear from owners whose new vehicle has gone back to the dealer for the same concern, even though each visit produced a different explanation or repair. We compare the dates, mileage, technician notes, warranty coverage, and correspondence tied to those visits. This is worth looking at now rather than letting repair paperwork pile up because every shop visit can add a date, mileage reading, diagnosis, or notation that changes how the vehicle history reads.

Warning lights and repeat repairs

Owners often call us about an engine warning light that returns after the dealer clears a code, replaces a sensor, or updates vehicle software. Other complaints include a transmission that jerks during acceleration, brakes that squeal or shake, a backup camera that cuts out, and a vehicle that loses power or stalls at a stoplight. We also review records involving a battery that repeatedly dies, doors or windows that stop working, and a touch screen that freezes while the vehicle is running.

A repair order may describe the same driveway complaint with different words, such as hesitation, loss of power, rough idle, or intermittent stalling. We read the owner concern, the technician findings, and the final work line rather than relying only on a brief description from the service lane. Similar sounds or warning lights can come from separate components, so each repair history needs its own answer.

Repair record review

During the first conversation, we ask about the vehicle, the main defect, the dealer visits, and whether the concern appeared while warranty coverage was active. We can usually clarify the basic sequence of events from the owner’s account, including whether the same symptom returned after a repair. Questions about exact dates, mileage, diagnostic codes, and the wording on a repair order need the paperwork in front of us.

We gather the purchase or lease agreement, repair orders, invoices, dates the vehicle entered and left the shop, and correspondence with the dealer. We place the shop dates beside the mileage entries and compare the reported concern with the technician’s notes, parts replaced, and warranty records. We then identify missing documents, repeated complaints, and changes in the dealer’s explanation before discussing the next stage of the matter.

Repair attempts and warranty dates

The number of repair attempts matters because repeat visits can show whether a dealer addressed the same defect or worked on a different component each time. The days a vehicle remained at the dealership also matter, especially when an owner could not use the vehicle while a technician waited for a part, tested an intermittent fault, or completed another repair. We read the check-in and check-out dates on every repair order instead of estimating time from memory.

Written complaints carry weight because the service advisor’s description may show what the owner reported at each visit, even if the technician could not duplicate the symptom. We also check whether the defect was reported while the warranty was running, whether the vehicle was bought or leased, and who retains the dealer emails, text messages, and service invoices. Those records help us assess whether the repair history has enough documentation to warrant a closer review.

Repair orders and VIN

Before calling, owners can pull every repair order and invoice from the glove box, service folder, dealer portal, or email account. Each repair order commonly shows the vehicle identification number, the mileage at check-in, the date in and date out, the reported complaint, and the work performed. We recommend checking whether every dealer visit produced a document, including visits where the technician marked the concern as not duplicated.

The purchase or lease agreement often appears in the signed delivery folder or a lender email, while the VIN also appears on the windshield, registration, and insurance card. Dealer emails, text messages, and appointment confirmations can help match a complaint to a specific shop visit.
