Dealer Fraud Claims in Houston, TX

We review dealer documents, repair records, mileage, and sales statements.

Call (832) 245-5128 Ask a question

We often see owners return to the dealer first after finding a problem with the vehicle, the mileage, or the sales paperwork. That is a sensible step: the sales desk or service department may have an explanation, a repair order, or a document that clears up the concern. The conversation can stop helping, however, when a verbal explanation does not match the purchase agreement, listing, text message, or condition of the vehicle.

We look at the paper trail alongside what happened at the dealership and after the sale. A dealer fraud concern is worth examining now when the purchase documents, advertisements, correspondence, and vehicle condition can still be matched to one another. Waiting can make it harder to locate the version of a listing, identify the person who made a statement, or connect a repair visit to the concern raised at the sale.

Mileage mismatch and hidden damage

We hear concerns such as, “The mileage does not match what I was told,” “The vehicle had prior damage,” or “The warning light came on right after I drove it home.” Other owners report that the dealer described a repair as complete, but the same transmission noise, brake vibration, water leak, or engine warning returned after the sale.

We also review situations involving a promised warranty, an added product on the sales contract, a vehicle history report, or a trade-in value that does not line up with the documents signed in the finance office. A repair order may show a prior complaint that was not discussed, while a purchase agreement may list equipment or coverage differently from the sales conversation. Similar driveway complaints can lead to very different questions once we compare the mileage record, repair history, and signed paperwork.

Purchase file review

We begin a case review by listening to the sequence of events: what the dealer represented, what documents were signed, when the vehicle first showed a problem, and what happened during each return visit. During the first conversation, we can usually identify the vehicle, the transaction type, the central concern, and which records are available. Questions about the exact wording of a representation, the condition of the vehicle at sale, or a prior repair generally require the paperwork in front of us.

We then gather and read the purchase or lease agreement, buyer's order, financing papers, repair orders, vehicle history materials, warranty documents, and correspondence with the dealer. We check the dates in and out of the shop against the mileage on each repair order and compare the listed equipment, disclosures, and charges against the sales paperwork. Text messages, emails, screenshots, and advertisements can help us place a dealer statement beside the document that followed it.

Repair attempts, warranty, and purchase file

The number of repair attempts can matter because repeated visits for the same warning light, steering pull, electrical failure, or engine symptom may create a clearer record than a single complaint. We look for the concern written in the customer statement, the technician findings, the repair performed, and whether the same symptom appears on a later repair order. A visit that was never written down may still matter to the overall account, but a dated service document gives us more to compare.

We also examine how long the vehicle stayed out of service, whether the fault was reported while warranty coverage was active, and whether the vehicle was bought or leased. The purchase agreement and lease agreement can contain different disclosures, payment terms, and product descriptions, so we need the version actually signed. Who holds the records also matters: the dealer may have inspection documents, the service department may have internal notes, and the owner may have messages or photographs that preserve the condition of the vehicle.

Repair orders and purchase agreement

Before calling, locate the purchase or lease agreement, buyer's order, and every repair order from the dealer or another shop. These documents are often stored in the finance packet, glove compartment, email inbox, manufacturer app, or service department portal. The purchase papers may show the VIN, mileage at sale, listed options, add-on products, and signatures from the transaction.

Check each repair order for the date the vehicle entered and left the shop, the mileage, the stated complaint, and the work performed. Save any text messages, emails, screenshots of an advertisement, vehicle history report, or photograph showing damage, a warning light, or the odometer.
